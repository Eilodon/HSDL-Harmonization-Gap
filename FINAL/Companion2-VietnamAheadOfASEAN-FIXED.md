# Vietnam Ahead of ASEAN: Structural Lessons from Southeast Asia's First Binding AI Law

*A companion paper to "Two Failure Modes Require Architectural Change: A Formal Harmonization Gap Analysis of the EU AI Act, Vietnam's AI Law, and the ASEAN AI Governance Guide" (the "main paper"), translating its formal findings for a legal and governance-policy audience.*

---

## Abstract

Vietnam's Luật 134/2025/QH15, effective 1 March 2026, is Southeast Asia's first binding national AI law — arriving while ASEAN's own AI Governance Guide remains voluntary and the bloc continues to debate whether, and how, to move toward binding regional rules. This paper uses a formal divergence analysis (developed in a companion technical paper) as empirical grounding for a governance-audience argument: Vietnam is not simply "catching up" to the EU AI Act, it is in places *stricter* than the EU in formal legal force, and it is now well ahead of ASEAN's own voluntary baseline on every tracked obligation. We translate four structural mismatch types — regulatory silence, strictness gaps, classification-scheme incompatibility, and who-is-responsible mismatches — into plain governance language, and turn them into concrete recommendations for ASEAN's binding-governance transition, for Vietnam's own drafting choices, and for deployers operating across all three regimes.

---

## §1 Introduction

Vietnam's AI Law took effect on 1 March 2026; its implementing decree, Nghị định 142/2026/NĐ-CP, followed on 30 April 2026. Compliance is staged rather than immediate — an 18-month transition for AI systems in healthcare, education, and finance (running to September 2027), and a 12-month transition for other systems (to March 2027) — but the legal force of the obligations themselves, the subject of this paper, is not staged; only the deadline for demonstrating compliance is. Both arrived while the Association of Southeast Asian Nations is in the middle of an unresolved debate about whether to move beyond its 2024 ASEAN Guide on AI Governance and Ethics — a voluntary instrument — toward binding regional rules. That debate has institutional momentum behind it: the ASEAN Digital Economy Framework Agreement (DEFA), a legally binding trade instrument covering data flows, cybersecurity, and AI governance across the bloc, is expected to reach signature by the end of 2026, and commentary on ASEAN's 2026 chairmanship has explicitly flagged AI governance fragmentation as a gap the bloc has "yet to address." Vietnam did not wait for that regional process, and it is not alone for long: Indonesia is finalizing its own mandatory AI requirements and Malaysia is weighing dedicated AI legislation, making 2026 a year in which several ASEAN members move roughly simultaneously, with Vietnam first across the binding-law line. Vietnam is now the reference point other ASEAN members are watching — and, this paper argues, the reference point is more interesting, and less simply derivative of the EU, than the surrounding commentary generally assumes.

The conventional account of AI regulation's spread is that the EU AI Act sets a de facto global floor, and other jurisdictions converge toward it, with Southeast Asia lagging furthest behind. A formal, rule-by-rule comparison of the EU AI Act, Vietnam's AI Law, and the ASEAN Guide — encoding all three into a shared, checkable representation rather than comparing them by reading and prose — overturns part of that account. At the lowest risk tier, Vietnam's law is formally *stricter* than the EU's: a universal human-control principle binds in Vietnam where the EU AI Act simply does not address the case. And against ASEAN's own Guide, Vietnam's law is now stricter across essentially every tracked obligation — unsurprising in one sense, since Vietnam's instrument is binding and ASEAN's is not, but consequential in a more specific sense this paper develops: the gap is total enough, and recent enough, that almost no existing comparative literature has registered it yet.

This paper's job is not to re-derive those results — that is the companion technical paper's job — but to state them for an audience that needs to act on them: ASEAN officials weighing the DEFA-era transition to binding rules, Vietnamese policymakers deciding whether to defend or soften specific provisions as international harmonization pressure builds, and deployers who need to know, in plain terms, what changes when a compliance program built for one of these three regimes is ported to another.

## §2 What the Numbers Show

**Four kinds of mismatch, not one.** A rule-by-rule comparison of two AI laws can fail to line up in four structurally different ways, and treating them as one undifferentiated "gap" obscures which mismatches a quick amendment can fix and which cannot.

- *Regulatory silence*, where one regime imposes an obligation and the other simply has no rule addressing the scenario at all — not a lenient rule, no rule. This is the easiest kind of gap to close: Vietnam's own Điều 10 Khoản 5, once correctly accounted for, closes exactly this kind of silence for high-risk systems after they reach the market.
- *Strictness gaps*, where both regimes have a rule, but one is more binding than the other. These are also fixable by ordinary text amendment — raise or lower a bindingness label — though "fixable" does not mean "should be fixed": as §4 below argues, some of Vietnam's strictness choices are better defended as policy than quietly converged away.
- *Classification-scheme incompatibility*, where the two regimes' systems for sorting AI systems into risk categories cannot be translated into each other at all, because they organize the underlying space along genuinely different axes. The EU's Annex III lists named use-cases as automatically high-risk; Vietnam's catalog instead conditions some categories on factors like whether a human approval step exists, and groups others by potential harm scale rather than by use-case. No amount of relabeling thresholds reconciles two classification schemes that are asking different questions.
- *Obligor mismatches*, where two regimes agree completely on how binding an obligation is, but disagree on *who* must satisfy it — a state inspector in one regime, the AI provider itself in the other. Two regimes can look identically strict on paper while assigning the actual duty to entirely different kinds of actor, and "equally binding" routinely gets read as "equivalent obligation" when it is not.

The first two are repairable by drafting; the last two are not — they require an architectural decision about how a law is structured, not an edit to a threshold.

**The reversal, and why it is not a rounding error.** At minimal risk — the tier the EU AI Act mostly ignores — Vietnam's Điều 4 Khoản 2, a universal human-control principle, binds regardless of risk tier. This is not a one-off artifact of a single sweeping clause: the same direction of reversal recurs independently in Vietnam's incident-reporting rules, where a notification duty triggers at a risk tier the EU's parallel rule does not yet reach. Two independent provisions producing the same reversal is evidence of a structural feature of how Vietnamese law allocates obligations across risk tiers, not a coincidence traceable to one broadly worded clause. Read as a percentage across the six obligation areas this analysis tracks, Vietnam is the *stricter* regime in roughly 60% of tracked configurations — under the baseline way of mapping the EU's risk tiers onto a shared schema that this analysis uses as its primary reading. That figure is not robust to the mapping choice itself: the EU AI Act does not specify how its own "Medium" risk tier should align with a merged classification, and an equally defensible alternative mapping reverses the direction, making the EU the more frequently stricter regime of the two instead. The durable lesson is therefore the weaker one — which regime is laxer is not predictable from country-level reputation, full stop, not a fixed "60%" or "twice as often" ratio a firm could plan around. It still inverts the usual "firms flee to the laxer Global South jurisdiction" story often enough to be worth naming — just as a direction worth checking case by case, not a number to rely on.

One caveat the numbers do not by themselves resolve: bindingness is not the same axis as operational specificity. Vietnam's universal principle states an outcome — humans must retain control — without spelling out a mechanism. The EU's parallel high-risk provision states a comparable outcome *and* adds at least one concrete mechanism (a stop-button-style override procedure) plus a quantified verification step for certain biometric systems. Both are equally "binding" in formal legal force; whether a declared outcome or a specified mechanism does more in practice is a separate, currently unmeasured question. "Vietnam is stricter" is a defensible, code-verified claim about legal force. "Vietnam is more protective in practice" is a different claim this analysis does not make.

**Against ASEAN, the gap is closer to total.** Comparing Vietnam's law to ASEAN's own voluntary Guide — not to the EU — produces the largest divergence of the three pairings: on every tracked obligation, Vietnam's binding requirement exceeds whatever ASEAN's Guide says, because ASEAN's Guide mostly does not bind anything. This is, in one sense, exactly what "binding law vs. voluntary guide" predicts. What is less predictable, and more useful to name explicitly, is that this is almost certainly the least-studied pairing of the three: existing comparative AI-governance literature concentrates overwhelmingly on the EU–US–China triangle, treats Southeast Asia as a descriptive aside if at all, and — because nearly all of it predates March 2026 — could not have registered Vietnam's specific transition from ASEAN's baseline to binding national law even if it had wanted to.

## §3 What Should Change — For ASEAN

ASEAN's transition from a voluntary Guide toward binding governance, whenever it happens, will force a choice the Guide currently avoids by being principle-based rather than rule-based: which factors carve up the regulatory space. The EU organizes almost entirely around a risk tier; Vietnam organizes around a wider mix of risk tier, lifecycle stage, whether the system interacts with a human, and other operational facts. These two organizing schemes cannot be reconciled after the fact by amending thresholds — they cut along different axes entirely, the same incompatibility this paper's classification-mismatch category describes. ASEAN does not need to resolve this today, while its Guide remains voluntary. It does need to resolve it *before* finalizing binding text, because the choice of organizing axis, once made binding, becomes exactly the kind of architectural commitment a later amendment cannot cheaply undo. A structural compatibility check against the architectures already binding on the bloc's largest economies — Vietnam's multi-axis structure and the EU's tier-based one, since EU compliance already matters for ASEAN-based exporters — is a concrete, checkable design test ASEAN can run before drafting, not after.

Obligor allocation deserves the same explicit attention, for a more specific reason: two regimes can describe textually "equivalent" obligations while quietly assigning the duty to different kinds of actor — a state inspector here, the provider's own self-monitoring there. If ASEAN's eventual binding instrument is meant to support mutual recognition with EU- or Vietnam-aligned obligations, that recognition needs its own clause on *who* is responsible, separate from and in addition to a clause on how binding the obligation is. Equal bindingness language is not equal-obligor language, and a recognition regime that only checks the former will produce false equivalences.

Finally, even ASEAN's own existing taxonomy will face a EU-style compatibility problem the moment it tries to interoperate with risk-tier-based instruments: ASEAN's harm-type categories (societal, economic, environmental, security, human-rights, ethical) are an unordered partition, not a severity scale, and no single fixed mapping can convert one into the other without losing information a regulator would need. This is worth resolving by design, in any future binding ASEAN instrument, rather than discovering it after adoption — exactly as has already happened, independently, in the EU–Vietnam comparison.

## §4 What Should Change — For Vietnam

The minimal-risk reversal — Vietnam binding universally where the EU is silent — is worth Vietnam stating as a deliberate policy choice, not quietly converging away as international scrutiny of the new law increases. There are two coherent positions here, and Vietnam should pick one explicitly rather than let the question be settled by drift. It can defend the universal human-control principle as intentional human-centric policy that distinguishes its regulatory philosophy from the EU's tier-gated approach — a genuine point of comparative advantage in international AI-governance discourse, not a drafting oversight to apologize for. Or, if regulatory convergence with the EU is a stated national goal, Vietnam should recognize what that convergence actually requires: *loosening* the universal principle's bindingness, not further tightening other high-risk provisions, since the gap in question runs in the opposite direction from the one most "catching up to the EU" commentary assumes.

Vietnam's post-market supervision provision (Điều 10 Khoản 5) also needs one clarifying sentence it currently lacks. Properly accounted for, this provision closes an apparent gap relative to the EU's continuous risk-management mandate — but it closes it via state inspection, not via a continuous provider-side monitoring duty of the kind the EU actually requires. These are not the same obligation in different words; they are different obligations held by different actors. The law does not currently say whether periodic state supervision is meant to *substitute for* a continuous provider self-monitoring duty or to *supplement* one Vietnam has not yet enacted. A single sentence resolving that ambiguity would close a question this analysis can surface but cannot answer on Vietnam's behalf.

Finally, the mandatory-certification catalog under Điều 13 Khoản 4 is doing more work than its classification function alone: whichever systems the final version names will also be the systems where third-party certification is mandatory rather than optional, since the dominant track elsewhere in the law lets providers self-assess. That is a second, independent reason — beyond the classification-comparability question this catalog already raises — to finalize and publish it promptly rather than leave it in draft.

## §5 What Should Change — For Deployers

The single most actionable lesson for a deployer operating across the EU, Vietnam, and ASEAN is this: a compliance file built to satisfy one regime's "binding" risk-management requirement does not automatically satisfy another regime's requirement at the same nominal bindingness level, because *who* must do the work can differ even when *how strict* the requirement does not. Vietnam's event-triggered control duty sits with the deploying organization; the EU's parallel continuous risk-management duty sits with the provider. A file built around provider-side continuous monitoring, however thorough, is evidence of the wrong thing if what a Vietnamese regulator is checking for is deployer-side event response. Porting a compliance program from one jurisdiction to another should trigger a check against *both* axes — strictness and responsibility — not strictness alone.

Reading the strictness gap as a regulatory-arbitrage signal cuts in a direction the popular narrative gets backwards for this specific pairing — under the baseline classification choice this analysis uses, the EU AI Act is the *laxer* jurisdiction roughly twice as often as Vietnam's newer law is. That ratio is not itself robust: an equally defensible alternative way of classifying EU risk tiers reverses which regime comes out laxer more often, for the same six obligation areas. A deployer who assumed Vietnam was uniformly the lighter-touch option, a compliance team that assumed the opposite, or either one anchoring on this paper's "twice as often" figure as a fixed number, would all be making the same underlying mistake. The country-level intuition ("the EU is strict, developing markets are lax") is not a reliable substitute for checking the actual obligation in question — and neither, by itself, is an unhedged percentage.

## §6 The Formal Tool as Policy Instrument

The encoding this paper's findings rest on is not a one-time research artifact whose value ends with this paper's seven reported obligation pairs. It is a reusable specification: a deployer with a concrete system — a specific risk tier, sector, lifecycle stage, and the other operational facts the encoding tracks — can evaluate that system's obligations under any two of the three regimes directly, rather than waiting for a future paper or legal opinion to cover their specific configuration. That is a lower-cost, more immediately actionable use of this kind of formal comparison than any single finding it produces: the finding tells a reader what is generally true; the tool tells a specific deployer what is true for them.

This matters concretely for the 2026–2027 compliance window Vietnam's law opens. A multinational deploying a single AI system across EU, Vietnamese, and Singaporean or Indonesian operations, for example, does not need to wait for a bespoke comparative legal opinion to know whether its existing EU compliance file transfers: the same dimensions this paper's schema tracks — risk tier, sector, lifecycle stage, whether the system interacts directly with a human, whether serious harm has been discovered — are exactly the facts a compliance team already holds about its own system. Running those facts through the same kind of check this paper runs at the aggregate level is, in principle, a same-day exercise rather than a multi-week legal review, for the six obligation areas this analysis covers.

## §7 Conclusion

Vietnam's AI Law is not simply an EU AI Act translated into Vietnamese with a time lag. On the formal evidence developed here, it is in places a stricter regime than the EU's own, and it is now substantially ahead of ASEAN's own voluntary baseline at a moment when the bloc is actively weighing whether and how to follow. That combination — stricter than the presumed global leader on one axis, far ahead of one's own regional bloc on another — is an unusual enough position that the surrounding commentary's default frame, "Vietnam catching up," undersells what is actually happening. Whether ASEAN treats Vietnam's law as a cautionary tale about architectural lock-in or as a template worth adapting is a political choice this paper does not make for the bloc. What this paper does argue is that the choice should be made with a structural understanding of where the four kinds of mismatch actually sit — silence, strictness, classification, and obligor — because only two of the four are fixable by the kind of text amendment a fast-moving regional negotiation favors.
