# When Lex Specialis Fails: Aggregation Semantics for Obligor Sets in Multi-Rule AI Regulatory Policies

*A companion paper to "Two Failure Modes Require Architectural Change: A Formal Harmonization Gap Analysis of the EU AI Act, Vietnam's AI Law, and the ASEAN AI Governance Guide" (the "main paper").*

---

## Abstract

When several rules in a regulatory policy fire on the same case, two questions arise: how strict is the resulting obligation, and who must satisfy it? The main paper shows the first question — bindingness aggregation — is algebraically invariant in its three-jurisdiction encoding: at most one obligation level ever co-fires, so any reasonable aggregation rule (union, *lex specialis*, unanimity) returns the same answer. The second question — obligor aggregation — is not invariant, and this paper develops the theory of why. We formalize two candidate specificity orders for resolving multi-rule obligor sets by *lex specialis* (the more-specific rule's named actors win, rather than the union of every co-firing rule's named actors), show that the semantic order is *structurally undefined* on Group 1 of the main paper's encoding — not occasionally, but on 100% of its multi-rule contexts — and prove this is an instance of a general theorem: whenever the co-firing rule set forms an antichain under the semantic specificity order, *lex specialis* aggregation is INDETERMINATE by construction, independent of which obligors any individual rule names. A second case, Group 4, is *not* an antichain under the weaker syntactic order and so admits a *lex specialis* answer — one that disagrees with union aggregation on 288 contexts, in the opposite direction from Group 1's indeterminacy. Together, the two cases show that *lex specialis* for obligor sets is not simply "harder to compute" than union aggregation; it is a different aggregation rule whose applicability is itself a structural, checkable property of a policy's rule set.

## §1 Introduction

A regulatory policy expressed as a set of conjunctive rules will, in general, have contexts where more than one rule applies. The main paper's bindingness layer (`β`, aggregated by `max`) and obligor layer (`ω`, aggregated by union) both need an answer to "what happens when rules co-fire" — but the two layers face genuinely different versions of that question. `B`, the bindingness scale, is a finite total order, so *lex strictior* (the strictest applicable level governs) is uncontroversial as a conservative compliance posture, and the main paper proves it is in fact the *only* answer its encoding can produce: every co-firing rule pair in that paper's six obligation-domains carries an identical bindingness label, so the aggregation choice is observationally vacuous (Corollary H5.2, main paper §5.9). `O`, the obligor set, has no such order — `Regulator` is not "stricter" than `Provider`, the two are categorically different kinds of duty-bearer — and unlike bindingness, co-firing rules in the main paper's encoding frequently name *different* obligors. Union aggregation (every named obligor across every firing rule is jointly responsible) is the main paper's canonical choice, but it is not the only candidate: a *lex specialis* aggregation — the most specific applicable rule's obligors govern, and less-specific co-firing rules are superseded — is a live alternative with real legal-theoretic pedigree (§2 below), and the two rules can disagree.

This paper asks the question the main paper flags but does not resolve: under what structural condition is *lex specialis* aggregation for obligor sets well-defined at all, and when it is, how does it compare to union aggregation? We answer in three parts. §3–§4 recall the main paper's obligor apparatus and extend it with two candidate specificity orders — a principled semantic order (`Rᵢ` is at least as specific as `Rⱼ` iff every context satisfying `Rᵢ` also satisfies `Rⱼ`) and a weaker syntactic proxy (more constrained dimensions counts as more specific) — together with a *lex specialis* obligor function that returns an explicit `INDETERMINATE` sentinel rather than silently falling back to union when no unique most-specific rule exists. §5 states and proves the paper's main theoretical contribution: the **Antichain Indeterminacy Theorem**, which shows that *lex specialis* is INDETERMINATE under the semantic order whenever the co-firing rule set forms an antichain (no rule's support is a subset of another's) — a purely structural, checkable condition on the policy, independent of which specific obligors any rule names. §6 applies this to the main paper's Group 1 (where the four-rule VN mini-regime turns out to be an antichain on 100% of its multi-rule contexts, making `ω_union` the *only* well-defined aggregation choice there) and contrasts it with Group 4 (which is not an antichain under the syntactic order, so *lex specialis* returns an answer — one that disagrees with union on 288 contexts, in a direction union aggregation alone would not surface).

## §2 Related Work

*Lex specialis derogat legi generali* — the more specific rule displaces the more general one — is one of the oldest interpretive maxims in legal theory, but its formal treatment in normative-systems research is comparatively recent and almost entirely about *which rule wins*, not about *whether a winner is structurally guaranteed to exist*. Governatori and Rotolo's defeasible-logic program treats rule priority as a first-class object: their account of legal abrogation and annulment in Defeasible Logic models how a normative system's rule set changes when one provision supersedes another, and the broader Governatori–Rotolo–Boella–van der Torre line on legal compliance and interpretation formalizes related Roman-law maxims about how strictly a norm's stated scope binds its application. LegalRuleML, the OASIS interchange standard this body of work feeds into (Palmirani, Governatori, Rotolo, and collaborators), provides defeasibility and priority as representable primitives precisely so that a *lex specialis*-style override can be expressed and exchanged across reasoning systems.

What this literature does not supply — because its motivating use case is typically a single jurisdiction's internally consistent rule set, where a legislator's drafting intent usually does settle priority — is a structural account of when *no* priority ordering exists between two co-firing rules at all, as opposed to when one exists but is merely undiscovered or contested. The deontic-logic and priority-based-conflict-resolution tradition more broadly tends to assume a priority relation is given, or computable from sources like temporal precedence or hierarchical rank, and asks how to reason once it is in hand. This paper's setting — three independently drafted instruments encoded onto a shared context schema, with no shared legislative history to settle priority by intent — exposes a prior question that intent-based approaches do not need to ask: given only the rules' *logical* specificity (subset containment of their firing conditions), is a *lex specialis* answer available at all? The Antichain Indeterminacy Theorem (§5) is an answer to exactly that question, stated independently of any particular policy's content.

## §3 Preliminaries: The Obligor Apparatus (recap)

This section recalls, without re-deriving, the definitions from the main paper's §2.9 needed below.

**Obligor Set (Def. H9).** `O = {Provider, Deployer, Regulator, ThirdPartyCertifier}`, a finite *flat* set — no natural total order, unlike the bindingness scale `B`.

**Labeled Regime with Obligor (Def. H10).** A triple `(P, λ, ο)` where `(P, λ)` is a bindingness-labeled regime and `ο: P → ℘(O)` assigns each rule the obligor set its text names (`∅` if the text names none).

**Obligor Function (Def. H11).** `ω_{(P,λ,ο)}(ctx) = ⋃{ο(Rᵢ) : Rᵢ ∈ P, ctx ∈ S(Rᵢ)}` — *union* aggregation, chosen because `O` has no order to take a `max` over: when multiple rules fire, every named obligor is simultaneously responsible.

**Two-Dimensional Output (Def. H12) and Obligor Gap (Def. H13).** `Φ(ctx) = (β(ctx), ω(ctx))`; `ObligorGap(A,B)` is the set of contexts where both regimes impose an obligation (`β_A, β_B > ⊥`) but on different obligor sets (`ω_A(ctx) ≠ ω_B(ctx))`.

**Proposition H13.1 (Dimension Independence).** `β`-equality does not imply `ω`-equality — proven by counterexample in the main paper, and the central motivation for treating `ω`'s aggregation question as worth a separate, careful treatment rather than inheriting `β`'s `max`-aggregation by default.

## §4 Specificity Orders and the Lex Specialis Obligor Function

**Definition 1 (Semantic Specificity Order, `⪯_sem`).** For rules `Rᵢ, Rⱼ` over the same `Ctx`:

```
Rᵢ ⪯_sem Rⱼ  ⟺  S(Rᵢ) ⊆ S(Rⱼ)
```

`Rᵢ` is at least as specific as `Rⱼ` iff every context satisfying `Rᵢ` also satisfies `Rⱼ` — the principled reading of "more specific": a narrower firing condition. By the main paper's Lemma 1, `S(R) = S_num(R) × S_cat(R)`, so the subset check is per-dimension, `O(k)` per pair and `O(|P|² · k)` for an entire policy, precomputable once. `⪯_sem` is a *partial* order: rule pairs whose supports are incomparable (neither contains the other) are common, not exceptional, in a multi-dimensional `Ctx`.

**Definition 2 (Syntactic Specificity Order, `⪯_syn`).** Let `dims(Rᵢ) ⊆ {risk_tier, sector, system_role, …}` be the `Ctx` dimensions `Rᵢ`'s body actually constrains. Define `Rᵢ ⪯_syn Rⱼ ⟺ |dims(Rᵢ)| ≥ |dims(Rⱼ)|` — a rule constraining more dimensions is treated as more specific. `⪯_syn` is a *total preorder* (any two integers compare), but admits ties, and it is only a proxy: more constrained dimensions does not entail a logically narrower firing set in general (a rule pinned to five dimensions can still have a larger support than a rule pinned to one, if the five-dimension rule's ranges are wide). We retain it because, unlike `⪯_sem`, it always returns an answer (up to ties) — and because pre-committing both orders before computing on any specific policy, rather than choosing post hoc, prevents selecting whichever order produces a more convenient result.

**Definition 3 (Lex Specialis Obligor).** Given a specificity order `⪯` (either of the above), define:

```
ω^LS_⪯(ctx) = {
    ο(R*)            if ∃! minimal element R* in {Rᵢ ∈ P : ctx ∈ S(Rᵢ)} under ⪯,
    INDETERMINATE    otherwise (no firing rules, or multiple minimal elements).
}
```

A minimal element under `⪯` is a firing rule with no strictly-more-specific firing peer. If a unique such `R*` exists, *lex specialis* assigns `ω` the obligor set of that uniquely most-specific rule; otherwise the result is the explicit sentinel `INDETERMINATE`, not a silent fallback to union. This refusal to fall back is deliberate, for the same reason the main paper treats Regulatory Void (Def. H3.5) and the totality fix on `τ_ASEAN` (Prop. H7.2) as first-class formal outputs rather than edge cases to paper over: an aggregation rule that is undefined on some inputs should say so, not guess.

**Theorem 1 (Lex Specialis Decidability).** *For any fixed `ctx ∈ Ctx` and any policy `P` whose specificity order `⪯` is precomputed, computing `ω^LS_⪯(ctx)` is decidable in time `O(|P| · k)`. Precomputing `⪯` itself costs `O(|P|² · k)` for `⪯_sem` and `O(|P| · k)` for `⪯_syn`, each one-time per policy.*

*Proof.* Identify firing rules: `O(|P| · k)`, as in the main paper's Theorem A. Filter `⪯`-minimal rules among them using the precomputed order: `O(|P|)` table lookups per rule, `O(|P|²)` total, but this is dominated by the firing scan once the order is precomputed. Check uniqueness of the minimal set: `O(|P|)`. Total per-`ctx` cost: `O(|P| · k)`, matching the main paper's Theorem A/A′. The precomputation cost amortizes across all `ctx` evaluations. □

## §5 The Antichain Indeterminacy Theorem

The decidability result above says *lex specialis* is computable; it says nothing about how often it is *defined*. This section's theorem identifies exactly the structural condition under which it is not — and shows that condition is independent of which specific obligors any rule names, depending only on the *shape* of the co-firing rule set under `⪯_sem`.

**Definition 4 (Co-firing set).** `F(ctx) = {Rᵢ ∈ P : ctx ∈ S(Rᵢ)}`, the set of rules firing at a given context.

**Definition 5 (Antichain).** `F(ctx)` is an *antichain* under `⪯_sem` iff for every pair `Rᵢ, Rⱼ ∈ F(ctx)` with `i ≠ j`, neither `S(Rᵢ) ⊆ S(Rⱼ)` nor `S(Rⱼ) ⊆ S(Rᵢ)` — no rule's firing condition is a subset of another's.

**Theorem 2 (Antichain Indeterminacy).** *If `F(ctx)` forms an antichain under `⪯_sem` and `|F(ctx)| ≥ 2`, then `ω^LS_sem(ctx) = INDETERMINATE`.*

*Proof.* By Definition 1, `Rᵢ ⪯_sem Rⱼ` iff `S(Rᵢ) ⊆ S(Rⱼ)`. If `F(ctx)` is an antichain, no `Rᵢ ∈ F(ctx)` satisfies `Rᵢ ⪯_sem Rⱼ` for any other `Rⱼ ∈ F(ctx)` (that would require `S(Rᵢ) ⊆ S(Rⱼ)`, excluded by the antichain condition), and symmetrically no `Rᵢ` is dominated by any other `Rⱼ`. A rule is `⪯_sem`-minimal in `F(ctx)` iff no other firing rule is strictly more specific than it; since no comparability relation holds at all between any two elements, every element of `F(ctx)` is vacuously minimal. With `|F(ctx)| ≥ 2`, this yields at least two minimal elements, so no *unique* minimum exists. By Definition 3, `ω^LS_sem(ctx) = INDETERMINATE`. □

**Corollary 1.** The finding that subset-based *lex specialis* is structurally undefined on 100% of Group 1's multi-rule contexts — reported in this paper's §6 below — is an instance of Theorem 2, not a free-standing empirical regularity requiring its own argument: it suffices to show Group 1's co-firing rule pairs form an antichain under `⪯_sem`, which §6 verifies directly.

**Corollary 2 (Contrapositive, informal).** A *lex specialis* answer under `⪯_sem` can exist only where the co-firing rule set is *not* an antichain — i.e., where at least one pair of co-firing rules stands in a genuine subset relation. This is a necessary, not sufficient, condition: even a non-antichain co-firing set can fail to have a *unique* minimum if it contains a more complex configuration than two rules; Theorem 2 characterizes the antichain case fully but does not exhaust every INDETERMINATE configuration.

Theorem 2's value is structural rather than computational: Theorem 1 already shows `ω^LS_⪯` is cheap to evaluate context-by-context, so one could always just *run* the computation and observe INDETERMINATE results empirically. What Theorem 2 adds is an explanation that does not require running anything: *because* Group 1's rules constrain pairwise-incomparable or disjoint dimension subsets (§6), its co-firing sets are antichains by inspection of the rule bodies, and INDETERMINATE follows without needing a brute-force check to confirm it case by case. This converts an empirical regularity into a provable structural property of the policy — in the same spirit as the main paper's Theorem C, which converts a partition-cover-incompatibility *observation* into a general decidability result.

## §6 Application: Group 1 (Antichain) and Group 4 (Non-Antichain) Contrasted

**Group 1 — confirming the antichain.** The main paper's Group 1 VN mini-regime has four rules: `art10_kh1` (fires on `{system_role, lifecycle_stage}`), `art10_kh2` (fires on `{modification_increases_risk}`), `art10_kh5a` and `art10_kh5b` (fire on disjoint values of `{risk_tier}`). Checking all six pairwise relations among the four rules (by direct inspection of their `Ctx`-dimension supports, confirmed by brute-force subset-check over the encoded policy): five pairs are *incomparable* in the strict sense required by Definition 5 — their supports overlap but neither contains the other — and one pair (`art10_kh5a` ↔ `art10_kh5b`) is *disjoint*, meaning it never co-fires and so never contributes a multi-rule context to check. The 816 multi-rule contexts (of 2,880 total) in Group 1 are covered entirely by the five incomparable, co-firing pairs — `(kh1,kh2)`, `(kh1,kh5a)`, `(kh1,kh5b)`, `(kh2,kh5a)`, `(kh2,kh5b)` — every one of which co-fires on a nonempty context set. Consequently every multi-rule `F(ctx)` in Group 1 is an antichain under `⪯_sem`, and Theorem 2 applies directly: `ω^LS_sem(ctx) = INDETERMINATE` on all 816 contexts, with no exceptions.

Two consequences follow. First, for Group 1, `ω_union` is not one defensible choice among several competing aggregations — by Theorem 2 it is the *only* algebraically well-defined choice among the candidates this paper examines. The main paper's worked Dimension-Independence instance (`ctx*`, where `ω_EU(ctx*) = {Provider}` and `ω_VN(ctx*) = {Regulator}`) is reportable at all only because union aggregation does not require a unique most-specific rule to exist. Second, `⪯_syn` — the weaker syntactic proxy — *does* return an answer at `ctx*`: `art10_kh1` constrains two dimensions against `art10_kh5a`'s one, so syntactic *lex specialis* selects `art10_kh1` and returns `{Provider}` alone, silently converting what union aggregation reports as a Provider/Regulator obligor mismatch into a Provider-only non-instance. This divergence between the two orders' outputs at the same context is exactly why both orders are pre-committed and reported, rather than one being quietly preferred: choosing `⪯_syn` would have made the main paper's headline obligor example disappear, not because the underlying legal facts changed, but because a less principled specificity proxy papers over a distinction the more principled one (correctly) cannot resolve.

**Group 4 — the contrasting non-antichain case.** Group 4's two VN rules are `art10_kh3_notify_classification` (fires on `{risk_tier, lifecycle_stage}`, two dimensions) and `decree142_art12_kh5_incident_report` (fires on `{serious_harm_discovered}`, one dimension). Their supports are semantically *incomparable* under `⪯_sem` (neither is a subset of the other — a context can satisfy one without the other and vice versa), so `⪯_sem` again returns INDETERMINATE wherever they co-fire (288 of the 2,880 contexts, at `risk_tier ∈ {Medium, High}` ∧ `lifecycle_stage = PreMarket` ∧ `serious_harm_discovered = true` — matching `art10_kh3`'s actual two-value condition per the main paper's §5.4; restricting to `risk_tier = High` alone would give only 144, half this figure). But under `⪯_syn`, `art10_kh3`'s two constrained dimensions outrank `decree142_kh5`'s one, so `art10_kh3` is the unique `⪯_syn`-minimal element: `F(ctx)` is *not* an antichain under the syntactic order, and *lex specialis* returns a definite answer, `ο(art10_kh3) = {Provider}`.

This answer disagrees with union aggregation. At those 288 contexts, `ω_union` reconstructs `{Provider, Deployer}` — exactly matching `ω_EU = {Provider, Deployer}` (Art. 12(1) Provider design duty plus Art. 26(6) Deployer retention duty) and so reporting *no* obligor gap there. Syntactic *lex specialis*, by contrast, keeps only `art10_kh3`'s `{Provider}`, which *does* differ from `ω_EU`, creating a gap union aggregation does not show. The direction of this disagreement is the opposite of what one might expect from Group 1's case: there, moving from union to a (syntactic) *lex specialis* reading shrank an apparent obligor mismatch into a non-instance; here, the same move *manufactures* a fresh 288-context mismatch where none existed under union. Aggregation choice can therefore move a per-group ObligorGap count in either direction — it is not the case that *lex specialis* is uniformly more conservative, or uniformly more permissive, than union.

**Why this matters beyond these two groups.** Group 1 and Group 4 are not idiosyncratic; they are the two qualitatively different cases Theorem 2 distinguishes. Any policy-comparison exercise that adopts *lex specialis* for obligor aggregation without first checking whether its co-firing rule sets are antichains risks one of two silent failures: reporting INDETERMINATE where a less careful implementation would have guessed (Group 1's risk, avoided here by the explicit sentinel), or reporting a confident-looking but order-dependent answer that a different — equally defensible — specificity proxy would reverse (Group 4's risk). Theorem 2 gives a cheap, structural pre-check — antichain or not, by direct inspection of rule supports, before running any per-context computation — for which risk a given policy faces.

## §7 Discussion and Conclusion

The main paper's Corollary H5.2 shows `β`-aggregation is invariant in its three-jurisdiction encoding because every co-firing rule pair happens to share a bindingness label — an algebraic fact about that specific encoding, not about bindingness aggregation in general. This paper shows the corresponding question for `ω`-aggregation has a different character: invariance is not just empirically false in the encoding (Group 1 and Group 4 already demonstrate that), but the *reason* it fails in Group 1 specifically is a provable structural property — antichain co-firing — independent of the particular obligors involved. That asymmetry between the two output dimensions is itself informative for anyone extending this kind of formal harmonization analysis to a new pair of jurisdictions: bindingness aggregation choice may turn out not to matter for a given encoding, but that has to be checked (as Corollary H5.2 does, by inspecting whether co-firing rules share labels); obligor aggregation choice should be assumed to matter unless an antichain check on the relevant co-firing sets says otherwise.

Two extensions are natural next steps, left open here. First, Corollary 2's contrapositive is only a necessary condition for *lex specialis* definedness; a full characterization of exactly which non-antichain configurations still yield a unique minimum (as opposed to merely *not* being a pure antichain) would sharpen the pre-check into a complete decision procedure rather than a one-directional flag. Second, this paper inherits the main paper's `⪯_syn` proxy largely because it always returns an answer, but Group 1's `ctx*` example shows that convenience comes at the cost of silently resolving a distinction `⪯_sem` correctly flags as unresolvable; a principled middle ground — perhaps a specificity order informed by which dimensions a statute's drafters evidently intended as restrictive, rather than a dimension *count* — is a legal-informatics question this paper's purely structural framing does not by itself answer.
